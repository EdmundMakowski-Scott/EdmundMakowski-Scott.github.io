Personal

Though my education at MSOE has been comprehensive and incredibly informative, there are bound to be some gaps, especially for someone like me who is constantly interested in both academia and industry. To fill these gaps, I have spent much of my free time during college learning the technical skills that I plan to use extensively in my engineering career.

CNC machining

The first technical skill I learned was how to use CNC machines. This interest was initially spawned by a need to manufacture a complicated component for my Lunabotics team. After researching the mildly absurd techniques I would need to master to create these components effectively and reliably, I decided that learning how to use a CNC would be easier.

Finite element analysis (FEA)

The second skill I learned was finite element analysis. This interest also came from the Lunabotics team: during the development of a particularly risky design, we decided that more advanced simulation was necessary. From this, my team and I were able to show that the stress at our arm and chassis connection would be low enough not to cause balance, stability, or durability issues within our system. That design then went on to be the one we chose to construct and implement!

OpenRocket

The third program I learned to use effectively was OpenRocket. This interest was purely of my own volition and was based on a general interest in understanding flight performance, stability, and power for various rocket designs. Using the program has helped me understand aerodynamics and energy transfer in a significantly more holistic way than I did previously.

Discrete element modeling (DEM)

The fourth skill I learned separately from my required education is discrete element modeling, or DEM. I consider it my most unusual and useful skill to date. I had heard about it in passing during my involvement with the Lunabotics competition, but my real interest did not begin until I met an industry rover analyst at Astrolab and discussed what direction the world of aerospace was moving in.

A conversation with an industry analyst

He was confident that aerospace was moving away from the pursuit of flight and toward the pursuit of excavation, and he noted that one of the most underrepresented and under-practiced forms of digital analysis was DEM. He reasoned that DEM was the next frontier of mechanical engineering and had been set aside thus far only because of the absurd computational requirements it demands. After a long conversation, and later a meeting between me, a colleague of mine, and the analyst, my colleague and I concluded that we must devote some time to this venture.

Current work

Since that meeting, we have researched different DEM tools and begun testing. We are compiling the digital results from those rudimentary simulations into one large machine learning (ML) program that can predict, with much less processing power, how lunar regolith interacts with solid-body mining and drive systems.

Though this project is still ongoing and results have varied, I remain confident that it is the most extraordinary project I have had the privilege to work on to date.
